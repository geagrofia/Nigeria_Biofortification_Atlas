Nigeria Crop Production

Introduction

There are number of sources of crop production data. The three main sources identified for Nigeria have different dates, and differing number of crops ( Table 1 ).

Methods

The data at state level need to be joined to the Nigeria States spatial dataset.

The stages are:

  1. Load the States dataset
  2. Load each crop database
  3. For each crop in the database join to the state spatial dataset and plot

MapSpam 2020

The MapSpam 2020 (You et al. 2014) data are provided as GeoTiff raster images.

NoteTotal area per grid cell

The spatial resolution of each cell in MapSpam 2020 is 5 arc minutes. This means that there is not a constant total area, but we can estimate the total area per cell for Nigeria:

Latitude side (5 arc-minutes north-south): 1° of latitude ≈ 111.2 km (nearly constant everywhere on Earth) 5’ = 5/60° → 111.2 × (5/60) ≈ 9.27 km

Longitude side (5 arc-minutes east-west): 1° of longitude shrinks with latitude by a factor of cos(latitude). At 10°N: cos(10°) ≈ 0.9848 1° longitude ≈ 111.2 × 0.9848 ≈ 109.5 km 5’ = 109.5 × (5/60) ≈ 9.13 km

Area of the grid cell: 9.27 km × 9.13 km ≈ 84.6 km²

Convert to hectares (1 km² = 100 ha): 84.6 × 100 ≈ 8,460 hectares

This means that no cell has more than 10,000 hectares, this knowledge is useful for plotting the crops.

Compare data sources

To help select the best data source it is necessary to compare the state statistics for each crop from the three sources.

Total crop area

NAERLS/FMAFS crop database

Add the harvested area for each crop and sum to obtain an estimate of the total crop area. I parsed all 17 crop tables in Section 12.0 (Rice, Maize, Sorghum, Millet, Cowpea, Groundnut, Soybean, Benniseed, Yam, Cassava, Cocoyam, Cotton, Ginger, Tomato, Onion, Okra, Plantain/Banana) and summed the “Land Area (Ha)” figures for each state across all crops it grows. The state-level sums total almost exactly to the sum of the national totals given in each table (a good cross-check), confirming the extraction is accurate.

Not every state grows every crop, so each state’s total reflects however many of the 17 crops were reported for it (ranging from 7 crops for Rivers up to 15 for Benue, Taraba, and Plateau). FCT and Bauchi top the list, largely driven by very large reported areas under crops like yam and cassava (whose land-area figures are much bigger than cereals per state).

Zamfara, Sokoto, and Katsina show the largest declines in total cropped area in 2024 vs 2023.

In addition to crops not reported these are the report’s own land-area estimates per crop (Table 12.1–12.17), not an independent GIS measurement — some of the same land may be double-cropped or intercropped, so this “total area” is a sum of reported cultivated hectares per crop rather than a literal footprint of unique land.

The comparison of crop areas among the sources shows some very large discrepancies among the sources. The most obvious difference is for the Federal Capital Territory. The NAERLS source gives a total crop area in excess of 2 million hectares, this in spite of the fact that the actual total area of the FCT (including urban areas, grassland, woodland etc.) is less than a million hectares.

Because NAERLS compiles its report using data sent in from individual State Agricultural Development Programmes (ADPs) and Ministries of Agriculture, errors usually trace back to a few specific systematic systemic issues:

  • The Double-Counting Trap: If a single field in the FCT was used to grow multiple crops simultaneously (intercropping, such as mixing maize, sorghum, and cowpeas on the same plot), the data collectors likely recorded the full size of that field for each individual crop. When those crop areas were summed together vertically, it generated an inflated “total crop area” that exceeded the physical size of the land.
  • Data Entry and Decimal Errors: A misplaced comma, extra zero, or basic typo during data aggregation can instantly turn a minor regional figure into a national peak.
  • Flawed Projection Baselines: Lacking a real-time satellite mapping infrastructure, local agencies often rely on rough estimates or outdated population multipliers to guess cultivated areas, which completely breaks down when applied to a rapidly developing urban territory like FCT.

The Worldcover map, in contrast, gives extremely low cropland cover estimates for the southern states. This may be because the classification algorithm doesn’t discriminate well between bushy semi-perennial crops - like cassava, or plantain - and shrub or tree cover, or because the cloud cover did not allow for sufficient satellite imagery.
